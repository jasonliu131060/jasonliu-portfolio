48 V to 12 V isolated forward converter

A 120 W isolated DC-DC converter I’m designing for Bruin Racing’s hydrogen fuel-cell Supermileage car.

Team
Bruin Racing · Supermileage Vehicle
Timeline
Jun 2026 – present
Role
Powertrain project engineer
Tools
LTspice · Altium Designer · STM32G474

This is my current project. I’ve simulated the design in LTspice and I’m now drawing the schematic in Altium. No hardware has been built yet.

Background

The car runs on a 48 V bus, and its low-voltage electronics need a separate, isolated 12 V supply. The same converter will also charge the 12 V battery. My target is 12 V at 10 A (120 W), switching at 200 kHz.

I chose an active-clamp forward topology with synchronous rectification. Besides the power stage itself, the converter needs gate drive across the isolation barrier, its own sensing and protection, and low-voltage supplies on both sides.

What I did

  • Designed the active-clamp power stage and simulated it in LTspice using Infineon’s OptiMOS6 150 V MOSFET models.
  • Modeled the PQ32/30 N97 transformer (6:4 turns, 180 µH magnetizing inductance) and swept leakage inductance and dead time (45–420 ns) to see how they affect switching and MOSFET voltage stress.
  • Designed the protection sensing circuits for an STM32G474: Kelvin-sensed current shunts, buffered voltage dividers, and NTC thermistors, read by the chip’s ADC and comparators.
  • Drew the schematic in Altium Designer across three sheets: the power stage, the gate drive and sync circuit, and the supplies.

Power stage

The transformer has a 6:4 turns ratio. The 6-turn primary is split into a 4-turn inner winding and a 2-turn outer winding, with the 4-turn secondary sandwiched between them. Interleaving the windings like this keeps the primary and secondary tightly coupled, which cuts down on leakage inductance.

All four MOSFETs (the main and clamp switches on the primary, and the forward and catch rectifiers on the secondary) have an RC snubber across them, 10 Ω in series with 220 pF. The snubbers are only there to absorb the voltage spikes and ringing from switching, not to clamp the voltage. The switches are driven by the gate drivers on the drive and sync sheet. A 10 mΩ shunt under the main switch measures primary current, and a 3 mΩ shunt after the 15 µH output choke measures output current.

Gate drive and sync

On the primary side, a UCC27735 half-bridge driver runs the main switch and the high-side clamp switch from the STM32’s PWM signals. Each gate has its own resistor and diode network, so turn-on and turn-off speeds can be set separately.

The synchronous rectifiers are on the secondary side, which has a separate ground from the primary. To pass the switching timing across that isolation, a UCC27524 gate driver takes the sync signal from the STM32 and acts as a 5 V buffer, giving a clean, strong pulse to drive a small sync transformer. On the secondary side, an LTC3900 synchronous rectifier controller detects the rising and falling edges of that pulse and switches the forward and catch MOSFETs to match.

Supplies

Each side of the isolation barrier needs its own low-voltage rails for the gate drivers, controller, and sensing amplifiers. On the primary side, a TPS62163 buck steps 12 V down to 5 V, and the 12 V rail also powers the primary gate driver. On the secondary side, two TPS7A25 LDOs take the converter’s 12 V output down to 10 V for the SR controller, and then down to 3.3 V.

Powering the primary side

This is the main problem I’m working on right now. The primary side needs its own 12 V rail. It powers the primary gate driver directly and feeds the 12 V-to-5 V buck. I want to get it from the 48 V input instead of adding a separate auxiliary supply.

My plan is to add an auxiliary winding to the transformer. Once the converter is switching, that winding can supply the 12 V rail. The catch is that it only produces power after switching starts, and switching can’t start until the controller has power. So I also need a small startup supply that runs off the 48 V input just long enough to get the converter going, and then the auxiliary winding takes over.

Design decisions

Turning off the catch rectifier at light load

At light load the output inductor runs in discontinuous mode. Keeping the catch MOSFET switched on in that mode made its voltage spikes worse, so I turn its gate drive off in DCM. At 1.5 A, that brought the simulated peak down from 74.4 V to 52.8 V.

Fault shutdown in hardware

Regulation runs in firmware on the STM32G474, but fault shutdown goes through the chip’s built-in comparators. That way the converter can shut off without waiting on the control loop.

Interleaving the transformer windings

I split the 6-turn primary into a 2-turn outer winding and a 4-turn inner winding, with the secondary between them. Keeping the windings close together like this reduces leakage inductance, which means less ringing and lower voltage spikes on the switches.

Planning for transformer tolerance

The core’s inductance can vary anywhere from 144 to 234 µH. I kept leakage inductance as a variable in the model so I can retune the dead times once a real transformer is wound and measured.
